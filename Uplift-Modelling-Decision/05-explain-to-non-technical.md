# Uplift-Modelling-Decision: Explained Simply

Repo: https://github.com/MelvTheGoat/Uplift-Modelling-Decision

*For a family member or a recruiter with no tech background.*

---

## What I built, in one sentence

A study that works out which customers a shop should e-mail when it can only afford to e-mail some of them: not the people most likely to buy, but the people who buy *because* they got the e-mail.

## The everyday comparison

Imagine you're handing out discount vouchers outside a supermarket, and you only have 300.
- Give one to someone **already walking in to do their big weekly shop**, and they'd have bought anyway. Wasted voucher.
- Give one to someone **walking past who wasn't planning to stop**, and it might change their mind. That's a voucher well spent.
- Give one to someone who **hates being stopped in the street**, and they might never come back. That's worse than nothing.

A normal approach gives vouchers to the people most likely to shop, which is mostly the first group. My approach tries to find the **second** group and avoid the third.

## What I did, step by step

1. **Practised on made-up data** where I secretly knew the right answer for every person, to check my methods actually work.
2. **Showed why the usual approach misleads.** On that made-up data, the usual method sent e-mails to **five times more** people who were put off by them.
3. **Used a real experiment** from an online clothing shop, where 64,000 customers were randomly split into "got an e-mail" and "didn't". That fairness is what makes the comparison trustworthy.
4. **Worked out the profit** of e-mailing different shares of customers.
5. **Tried hard to prove myself wrong** with several checks.
6. **Wrote a two-page memo** for a marketing director.

## What I found

- The e-mail does work.
- Choosing *who* gets it cleverly was worth about **$1,390** extra profit.
- But simply **being allowed to e-mail more people** was worth about **$3,170**. The budget limit mattered more than the clever choosing.
- One of my checks **failed**. When I scrambled the data on purpose, my method sometimes looked just as good. So I can trust the very top group it picks, but not the rest of its ranking. My recommendation is to run a proper test before relying on it.

## Why the honest result matters

Many analyses would stop at "our model beats random, roll it out". I reported that the evidence isn't strong enough yet, and designed the follow-up test instead. That protects a business from spending money on a result that might not be real.
